The token-classification approach to prompt compression reframes the problem as a per-token keep-or-drop decision. Instead of asking a generative model to rewrite the prompt more concisely, a small encoder-style classifier scores every token in the original input with the probability that the token is necessary to preserve the meaning of the passage as a whole. Tokens with low scores are dropped; tokens with high scores are kept. The output is reconstructed by detokenizing the kept tokens in their original order, which produces a compressed but grammatically imperfect result that downstream models can still parse correctly.

The training data for this classifier is constructed by taking natural-language passages, generating multiple compressed variants at different rates, and labeling each token as kept or dropped based on whether its presence in the input is necessary to reconstruct any of the variants above a quality threshold. The classifier itself is a BERT-style transformer with a token-classification head trained with a binary cross-entropy loss. The full model is roughly 110 million parameters and runs comfortably on CPU at inference time.

In practice, the approach works best on prose and narrative content. It works poorly on code, where dropping tokens can break syntax, and on dense structured data such as tables or JSON, where every character is meaningful. For those content types, a different transform is appropriate: a tree-sitter-based code skeletonizer that preserves signatures and replaces bodies with an ellipsis, or a structured-data summarizer that preserves the schema and samples the values.
